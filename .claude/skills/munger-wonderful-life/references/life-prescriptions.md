# Munger's Life Prescriptions (2007 USC Law Commencement)

Every prescription from "如何获得精彩人生？", each with Munger's own story and the exact lesson. Pull the ones that fit the person; let the story carry the point.

---

## 1. Deserve what you want（让自己配得上）
"To get what you want, the safest way is to try to deserve what you want." The golden rule — treat others as you'd want to be treated. People who live this win not just money and fame but *respect and trust*, which is the deeper reward. The counter-image: the scoundrel who dies rich and famous, whose funeral is full of people glad he's gone ("Well, his brother was worse"). Not the life you want. **Coaching move:** turn "how do I get X" into "how do I deserve X."

## 2. The learning machine（学习机器）
You don't go far on existing knowledge; keep learning for life or you won't achieve much. Berkshire's method for *this* decade won't work next decade, so Buffett had to become "a learning machine" — half his waking hours reading, most of the rest in one-on-one talk with trusted, able people. At every level Munger watched people get steadily better who weren't the smartest or hardest-working — "they go to bed every night a little wiser than when they got up." Whitehead: society only took off once it "invented the method of invention"; likewise you only progress once you learn the *method* of learning.

## 3. The multidisciplinary latticework
The joke professor's definition of the "legal mind" — able to consider one of two intertwined things while ignoring the other, and think that practical — is folly. The big ideas carry ~95% of the freight in each discipline, so take that 95% from *all* the major disciplines and make them thinking habits. Then *practice* them like a pianist. (This is the same latticework as the worldly-wisdom lecture, aimed here at living well, not just investing.) Caveat Munger flags: it's so useful that you'll often out-think higher-status people — which is dangerous (see #12).

## 4. Invert, always invert（逆向思考）
The country rube: "All I want to know is where I'm going to die, so I'll never go there." For a complex adaptive system or a human brain, turning a problem backwards often cracks it. To help India, ask how you'd most *harm* India, then avoid that. Algebra solves hard problems by working backwards; so does life.

## 5. The life-wreckers (found by inverting "what fails?")
- **Unreliability / breaking your word（言而无信）:** enough to undo every other virtue — "if you're unreliable it doesn't matter what your virtues are, you're going to crater." Build the habit of doing what you said.
- **Laziness.** Obvious, avoid it.
- **Envy/jealousy（妒忌）:** "It's not greed that drives the world, but envy" (Buffett). And it's uniquely stupid — a sin you get *no fun* out of. Big law firms pay senior partners equally partly to tamp down the chaos envy causes.
- **Resentment / self-serving bias:** don't let the subconscious conclude that what's good for you is good for the world, or use that to excuse stupid/evil behavior. But *do* allow others their self-serving bias — expecting everyone to purge it makes you the fool. Franklin's persuasion rule: "appeal to interest, not to reason" — the Salomon general counsel was legally and morally right but got himself and the CEO destroyed by appealing to duty instead of to the CEO's self-interest.
- **Self-pity（自怜）:** disastrous, near-paranoid, one of the hardest things to reverse. The friend's card: "Your story has touched me, I've never heard of anyone with as much bad luck as you." Self-pity never helps — not even when your child is dying. Train yourself out of it; it's a standard reaction, so escaping it is a big edge.

## 6. Work under people you admire
Avoid working for someone you don't admire or don't want to become — everyone is somewhat controlled by the authority figures who reward them. Young Munger's method: find people he respected and maneuver under them, without criticizing anyone. Life is more satisfying working for people you rightly admire.

## 7. Follow intense interest
Munger could force himself to be decently good at many things, but could only be *excellent* at what he was intensely interested in. So steer toward genuine interest where you can.

## 8. Maximize non-equality (give the best players the minutes)
Coach John Wooden told his five weakest players "you won't get playing time," gave nearly all the minutes to the top seven — who then learned more (learning machines!) and won more. You don't want your child's brain surgeon chosen by lottery among 50, or your plane designed egalitarianly. Let the most able, most learning-hungry people do the most.

## 9. Planck knowledge vs. chauffeur knowledge
Planck's chauffeur memorized the quantum-physics lecture and gave it; when a hard question came, he said "I'll let my chauffeur answer." Two kinds of knowledge: **Planck knowledge** (you did the work, you really understand) and **chauffeur knowledge** (fluent mimicry — nice hair, good voice, impressive delivery, no understanding). Strive to be the former and to detect the latter (Munger: "I just described all the politicians in the US"). Acquiring real wisdom is a **moral duty**.

## 10. Setbacks: the Epictetus attitude
You'll take heavy, unfair blows; some survive them, some don't. Epictetus: every misfortune, however bad, is a chance to practice and to behave well — fuel for self-improvement, never an excuse for self-pity. His self-written epitaph: "Here lies Epictetus, a slave, maimed in body, the ultimate in poverty, and favored by the gods." He's remembered as *favored by the gods* precisely because of that response. Pair with Housman: "I always think about trouble... so when trouble comes, I'm ready" — expecting trouble didn't make Munger unhappy; it helped him.

## 11. The seamless web of deserved trust（信任之网）
The best system isn't elaborate bureaucratic procedure — it's "a seamless web of deserved trust": reliable people who rightly trust each other, with few silly rules (the Mayo Clinic operating room; if surgeons ran on lawyer-grade procedure, more patients would die). "If your marriage contract is 47 pages, I suggest you not enter into it." Related: **thrift is the servant of duty** (Grandfather Munger saved so his widow wouldn't be destitute and so he could serve others — e.g., trading a third of his good assets to rescue his son's failing bank).

## 12. Hide your brilliance — or pay the price
Being right in a way that makes a higher-status person (even your boss) look foolish can trigger fierce retaliation. The senior partner's advice to Chuck: your job is to make the client feel like the smartest person in the room, then the senior partner the second-smartest, and only then show yourself. A good way to climb — but Munger mostly *didn't* do it, spoke his mind, and had "a hard time" for it. So: advise people to learn to conceal their wisdom where it matters, while being honest that Munger himself chose candor and accepted the cost.

---

Closing spirit: Munger ends by quoting *The Pilgrim's Progress*' Mr. Valiant-for-Truth in old age — "My sword I leave to him who can wear it." The point of the whole speech is to hand over hard-won rules to people who can use them. Coach in that spirit: offer, ground, let them choose.
