# Worldly Wisdom — The Latticework of Mental Models (普世智慧)

The full catalog of models from Munger's 1994 USC speech, by discipline, with the chapter's own anecdotes and the exact lesson each carries. Pull the models that **bite on the decision at hand** — not all of them. Munger: you need ~80–90 models, and only a few carry most of the freight.

The master rule: **you must have multiple models from multiple disciplines**, or "to the man with a hammer, every problem looks like a nail"（手里拿着铁锤的人，看每个问题都像钉子）, and you will distort reality to fit the one model you own. Hang experience on a *framework* of models; isolated facts banged together are useless.

---

## 1. Mathematics — the most basic, most under-used

- **Compound interest.** The foundational model. Most of the skill's tax section rests on it.
- **Permutations & combinations / decision-tree thinking.** The Fermat–Pascal system (settled in a year of letters, 1654). Harvard Business School's whole quantitative first year is "decision tree theory" — high-school algebra applied to real life, and students are amazed it works. The brain did NOT evolve to do this; it uses crude shortcuts. So you must *drill* it into a habit, the way a golfer learns a grip that isn't the natural swing. Buffett's edge: he automatically thinks in decision-trees and elementary permutations/combinations. Without it "you go through a long life like a one-legged man in an ass-kicking contest."
- **Expected value under the odds.** Carries straight into the pari-mutuel view of markets (see stock-picking).

## 2. Accounting — the language of business, and its limits

- Double-entry bookkeeping (a Venetian invention) is a great contribution to civilization and not hard to learn.
- **But it's only a crude approximation.** You must understand its *limits*. Depreciation schedules give pretty numbers but don't mean you understand the real economics of a jet engine's life.
- **Carl Braun** (C.F. Braun Engineering, built refineries on time): looked at the standard accounting and said **"This is bullshit,"** fired the accountants, and had his engineers design an accounting system that fit the actual business.
- **Braun's "five W's" communication rule** (it's really psychology): every communication must state **who** should do **what**, **where**, **when**, and **why**. Leave out the *why* twice and you're fired. Reason: telling people *why* makes them understand more deeply, weight it more, and comply more — even when the reason is obvious. (Mirror of acquiring wisdom by asking "why" repeatedly.)

## 3. Hard science & engineering — the most reliable models on earth

- Quality control (W. Edwards Deming's methods taken to Japan) is just the Fermat–Pascal math applied — basic high-school stuff dressed up in scary terms.
- **Backup systems**, **breakpoints**, and physics' **critical mass** are powerful, everyday-useful models.
- All cost–benefit analysis "is just high-school algebra with fancy terms."
- A plain professional magician will make you see things that aren't happening and miss things that are — a reminder that cognition is manipulable.

## 4. Biology / physiology — the ecosystem model

- We share the same genes, so physiology gives reliable models.
- **Treat a (partly) free-market economy as an ecosystem.** People resist this because robber barons abused "survival of the fittest," but the analogy is strong: **narrow specialists thrive in their niche**, just as animals do where they're adapted. This is the bridge into microeconomics and scale.

## 5. Psychology / the psychology of misjudgment（误判心理学）— most important, most neglected

- Perception misfires (the magician); cognition misfires *even more*. The brain has limited "wiring" and takes automatic shortcuts, so someone who understands the flaws can make you a patsy.
- **Munger's two-track analysis（双轨分析）:** (1) rationally, what *really* governs the interests involved? (2) what subconscious influences are forming — the mostly-useful-but-often-misfiring shortcuts? Always run both tracks.
- Munger calls his psychology toolkit **"误判心理学" — ~20 interacting principles**, learnable by a smart person "in a week," yet nobody taught him; he had to learn it painfully later. Even brilliant people make "really dumb" errors by ignoring it — Munger admits a few of his own in the prior years.
- **Social proof（社会认同）:** we're influenced — subconsciously and consciously — by what others do. "Everyone's buying it, so it must be good; I don't want to be the laggard." A scale advantage: Coke is everywhere.
- **Pavlovian association（巴甫洛夫联想）:** we reflexively resent the bearer of unwelcome news. **CBS's Bill Paley** surrounded himself with yes-men, ended up living in a cocoon of lies while the company rotted — "the last ten years were the Mad Hatter's tea party."
- **Denial:** chiropractors (and, Munger jokes, much of investment management) cope with their field's limits by flat denial. Don't adopt the denial model if you want a good life.
- Pascal: "The mind of man is both the glory and the shame of the universe." Half of Hitler's army were devout Catholics — with enough psychological manipulation, people do astonishing things.
- (The full treatment is the **Third Lecture, "聪明人为什么会犯错？"**. Note that pointer; don't fabricate its contents.)

## 6. Microeconomics — the heart of business analysis

### Scale advantages（规模优势）
The more you produce, the better you get (experience curve) — a huge edge. Forms:
- **Geometry / cost:** a bigger oil tank's steel grows as the square, volume as the cube → more capacity per unit of steel.
- **Advertising reach:** when color TV arrived, the 3 networks had ~90% of viewers; **P&G** could afford the hugely effective TV ads because it sold so much; small players literally couldn't.
- **Information advantage:** **Wrigley vs. Glotz gum** — in a remote place, you won't put an unknown gum (Glotz, 35¢) in your mouth to save 5¢ over Wrigley (40¢). Wrigley's reputation = a scale/information advantage.
- **Social proof:** Coke everywhere (see psychology).
- **Winner-take-all:** essentially one daily newspaper per US city — more circulation → more ads → more circulation.
- **Finer division of labor:** bigger scale lets each worker specialize and do their job better.
- Welch at GE: "**be #1 or #2 in every field or get out**" — ruthless but wealth-maximizing, because scale advantages are that powerful.

### Disadvantages of scale — "big isn't always better"（大未必就是好）
- **Specialization beats breadth:** Berkshire's broad business-travel magazine was killed by a dedicated corporate-travel one — narrower = more efficient, less wasted ink/paper. (*Dirt Bike* magazine's margins "make you drool.") Narrower niche, like the ecosystem, can be better.
- **Bureaucracy, bloat, corruption:** big firms get sluggish, buck-passing ("I handed it off, so it's done" — but AT&T's telegram isn't sent until it's *sent*). Mutual-non-interference pacts between managers, redundant layers, slow decisions. Worst of all: government bureaus with terrible incentives.
- **Sears vs. Wal-Mart:** Sears had massive scale advantages but drowned in bloat and rigid thinking; Walton's leaner, meaner, smarter, more fanatical system destroyed it.
- The remedy people find: decentralized small units + strong incentives/training (GE under the rare genius-plus-passion Welch).

### Moats / durable competitive advantage
- **Brand identity:** cereal makers earn 15–40% despite fierce promotion — brand (and learned restraint about chasing share) lets competition stay *rational*. Contrast the pure-commodity airline, whose competition is *destructive*.
- **Patents** (now more valuable since the specialized patent court is pro-patent), **trademarks** (always made people money), **franchises** (the only food stand in the airport = a little monopoly; pre-cable, a TV channel in a 3-channel city was a natural oligopoly).
- The open puzzle Munger admits: *why* some markets compete rationally (cereal) and others destructively (airlines; Pepsi/Coke in some markets vs. others) — "I don't have a perfect model." Honesty about model limits is part of the method.

### Competitive destruction & "surfing"
- **Competitive destruction（竞争性毁灭）:** the best buggy-whip maker is finished when cars arrive. Happens over and over.
- **Surfing（冲浪）:** the first mover who catches a new wave rides a long time — **NCR/Patterson** (the cash register "I'm going to go into the cash-register business"), Microsoft, Intel. But you must *stay on the wave*. **IBM surfed ~60 years, then got knocked off** when technology shifted — a vivid lesson in how hard tech businesses are (why Munger/Buffett avoid them).
- Berkshire generally doesn't invest in the surfers (outside its circle of competence), but that doesn't make *your* doing so irrational.

### Technology's two-step analysis (second-order effects)
- The salesman/your own staff say "this machine pays for itself in 3 years." They never do **step two**: how much of the saving reaches *you* versus flows to the customer.
- **Commodity textile mill:** Buffett hoped a 2×-better loom *wasn't* that good — because in a commodity business, all the productivity gain flows to the buyers; the owner keeps nothing. Buying "3-year-payback" gadgets for 20 years → <4%/yr. 
- **The only newspaper in Oshkosh:** new typesetting tech → the savings *stay with you*, because you have pricing power. Same technology, opposite outcome. Ask: **who captures the gain?**

## 7. Circle of competence（能力圈）

- Figure out what your aptitudes are; compete inside your circle. "If you play games where other people have the aptitudes and you don't, you're going to lose." Guaranteed.
- You won't be world tennis champ, but ~2/3 of people *can* become the best plumbing contractor in Bemidji — achievable with grit + intelligence + training. Build a circle slowly.
- Munger/Buffett sat out software, chips — not from genius but from honestly facing their knowledge gaps. Expanding the circle is very hard; **pretending it's wider than it is is the classic error.** Applies to Claude's own competence too — name the edge.

---

## Using the models in advice

- Pull only the models that engage the specific decision.
- Lead with the model, then the book's anecdote to sharpen it — the story illuminates, it doesn't decorate.
- Run **both tracks** (rational interests + subconscious influences) on any decision involving people or incentives.
- When a model doesn't fit, say so. Forcing a model is the hammer-and-nail error the whole framework exists to prevent.
