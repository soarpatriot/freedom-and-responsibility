# The Art of Stock Picking (选股的艺术)

Munger's framing: **stock picking is a small subdivision of the art of worldly wisdom.** Apply this playbook only *after* the worldly-wisdom pass (`mental-models.md`), and only to common stocks — Munger explicitly excludes FX, bonds, emerging markets, etc. ("I'm talking about picking American stocks.")

---

## 1. What is the market? — The pari-mutuel view（彩池投注系统）

- The first question is "what is the nature of the stock market?" — which leads to **efficient-market theory**. Munger: "the market is efficient *enough* that it's very hard for a smart, hard-working person to beat it by much" — but **partly efficient and partly inefficient**. The world's great economist held Berkshire as a big shareholder while his textbook taught that nobody can beat the market — he, like Pascal, hedged his bet.
- Munger's name for extreme efficient-market believers: **"bonkers"（神经病）** — a self-consistent theory that lets math-heavy people do pretty equations, but whose premises don't match reality. (Hammer-and-nail again: if you're great at higher math, you pick an assumption that uses it.)
- **The model: a race-track pari-mutuel.** Everyone bets; the odds move with the bets — exactly like a stock market. A light-weight, high-win-rate, well-posted horse will *probably* beat a bad heavy one — any idiot sees that. But the **odds** are 3-to-2 on the good horse and 100-to-1 on the bad one, so with Fermat–Pascal math it's genuinely hard to say which is the *bet* that makes money. **IBM (6× book in a hot market) vs. a troubled railroad (1/3 book):** everyone knows IBM's prospects are better — but *at the price*, which to buy is hard. That's why the market is hard to beat.

## 2. The 17% takeout — why you must be *a lot* better

- The track takes **~17%**. To win net, you must be not just better than other bettors but *much* better. A smart handicapper who beats the horses by 10%/season still *loses* after the 17%. Yet a few do win net.
- Munger's poker friend made a living on **harness racing** — a relatively *inefficient* market — by betting rarely, only on mispriced bets, and still clearing the ~17% takeout.
- **Stocks have a far smaller takeout** (bid-ask spread + commission, low if you don't churn). So a fanatical, disciplined, smart person *can* do better than average — but only by being extremely selective. ~50% land in the worst half, ~70% in the worst 70%; only some get the edge.

## 3. Hunt mispriced bets; bet big when you find one

- "The way to win is to work, work, work, work and hope to have a few insights."
- When a smart person finds a mispriced bet, **bet heavily**; the rest of the time, do nothing. Almost nobody in investment management actually does this — they diversify frantically and work harder, which Munger calls "total insanity."
- **You don't need many right calls.** Most of Berkshire's many-billions came from its **~10 best ideas** (and Buffett is smarter and more disciplined than Munger). Not 10 right calls total — but the big money came from ~10.
- **Buffett's 20-slot punch card:** imagine a card with only 20 punches = all the investments you may make in a lifetime. Under that rule you'd weigh each decision enormously and do far better. Not taught in business schools because it isn't conventional.
- The fishing-lure lesson: a man selling green-and-purple lures — "do fish take these?" — "Mister, I don't sell to fish." Many money managers sell to the *client's* appetite, not to what works (like selling more salt to a store already drowning in salt). But you can't fool the buyer of investment results over time.

## 4. Great business > great price (but price still matters)

- Methods that *don't* reliably make people rich: **sector rotation**（行业轮换）. Munger knows no one who truly got rich on it; the rich people he knows didn't do it.
- **Graham's classic method** — private-owner value: what would the whole business fetch if sold? Buy at **1/3 (or less) of that** → a big **margin of safety（安全边际）**, even if it's a lousy business run by a drunk. Graham's **"Mr. Market"**（市场先生）: a manic-depressive who shows up daily offering to buy or sell at varying prices — a gift, because you can choose to act or do nothing. These ideas served Buffett his whole life.
- **But the obvious cigar-butts disappeared** as everyone got smart — "the Geiger counter stops clicking in the ruins." Graham's followers just recalibrated the definition of "cheap" and kept going (the theory is that robust). The 1930s context — current assets truly belonged to shareholders; today, much of the balance sheet belongs to employees, and assets vanish when a firm downsizes (IBM's downsizing destroyed shareholder value — a "model" of it).
- **Berkshire's evolution → quality businesses.** Copying Graham verbatim wouldn't have produced Berkshire's record. Once they broke past Graham's limits (even buying at 2–3× book), they found stocks that were *still cheap* because of market-position momentum, superb managers, or systems. **Most of Berkshire's many billions came from these great businesses**, not the Geiger-counter bargains (which made the first few hundred million).
- **Long-run return ≈ the business's return on capital.** A 40-yr 6%-ROC business returns ~6% to you even if bought cheap; an 18%-ROC business rewards you even if you overpaid somewhat. **So the trick is to buy great businesses** — you're buying the momentum of their scale advantages.

## 5. Buying great businesses — ways and cautions

- **Catch them early** (buy Wal-Mart at Walton's IPO). Very tempting; Munger says if he were young he'd do it. But Berkshire can't — too much money, no small-company fits — and it's hard to join a great business once it's obviously great (competition is fierce).
- **A great business with a great manager** is ideal and often *foreseeable* — Welch vs. the Westinghouse guy; Eisner & Wells at Disney; Simon Marks (Marks & Spencer), Patterson (NCR), Sam Walton. Such people aren't that rare and can often be spotted.
- **But bet on the business before the manager.** If forced to choose, bet on the *prospects of the business*, not the brilliance of the manager — except the rare exceptional operator who can lift even a mediocre business.

## 6. Unused pricing power（尚未利用的提价能力）

- A rare, lucrative sub-pattern of the growth model: a business whose managers **could raise prices and haven't** — unexploited pricing power. "No-brainer" stocks.
- **Disney:** taking the grandkids is a unique, infrequent experience; it raised ticket prices far above inflation and attendance still grew.
- **See's Candies / Coca-Cola:** Berkshire raised See's prices early; Coke had unused pricing power plus great managers (Goizueta, Keough). "It's like finding money on the street — *if* you have the courage of your convictions."

## 7. The tax drag of churn

- **30 years, 15%/yr, taxed once at the end at 35% → ~13.3% after-tax.**
- The **same 15% taxed every year** at 35% → **~9.75%** (15% − 15%×35%). A **>3.5%/yr** gap that compounds into a staggering difference.
- Even a 10%/yr, 30-yr investment: taxed once at end → 8.3% after-tax; taxed yearly → 6.5%. So long holding adds roughly **1–3%/yr** quietly, plus fewer fees and less nonsense.
- **But over-reaching to avoid tax is a common catastrophic error.** Munger & Buffett "don't drill oil wells"; they pay their taxes. **"Munger's rule": whenever someone tries to sell you something with a 200-page prospectus and a big commission, don't buy it.**

## 8. The cancer-surgery model（癌症手术法）— GEICO

- One of the ~100 models worth remembering. Turnaround friends use it: stare at the mess, see if cutting the sick parts leaves a healthy business worth keeping; if yes, cut everything else; if not, let it go bankrupt. It usually works.
- **GEICO:** a superb core insurance business buried under self-inflicted nonsense (it thought making money meant it understood everything, took big losses). Cut the dumb parts, return to the great old business. "It made us a lot of money, almost too easily." A model worth looking for.

## 9. Landing an investment judgment

- Markets are like the pari-mutuel: the favorite more often wins, but betting the favorite rarely gives you an *edge*. Edge comes from a mispriced bet you understand.
- A good process: worldly-wisdom pass → is this inside the circle of competence? → is the business great and rational-competition (moat), or commodity/destructive? → who captures technology/productivity gains? → is there unused pricing power or a cancer-surgery cut? → is the *price* such that the bet, not just the business, is good? → if yes and conviction is high, bet big; else wait.
- **Always state the honest risk.** Buying great businesses is so obviously right it gets *overdone*: the 1950s–60s Nifty-Fifty hit 50–70× earnings and became a disaster when they fell off the wave (IBM). "Everything in life has risk." Give the recommendation, then the uncertainties that would change it.
- **Investment management, net, adds no value to clients in aggregate** (unlike plumbing or medicine) — most managers cope by denial. A very few add value, but not by brains alone: you need the discipline to bet big only on the rare understood opportunity.
